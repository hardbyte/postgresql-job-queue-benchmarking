Benchmark results · 2026-11-11 to 2026-11-13

Eight Postgres-backed queues, one host, the same scenarios.

Every system ran the same scenarios, one after another, against a fresh PostgreSQL 18.6 cluster capped at 4 CPUs, on one host, in November 2026. In saturation, the Postgres restart and many queues, every system had a pool of workers + 8 connections per process. In the other scenarios Awa and Absurd had that pool and the other systems their adapter's default (4 connections for Procrastinate, 10 for pg-boss). Absolute numbers depend on this host; the comparisons within a section are the point.

Maintained by Awa’s author.

Every adapter, scenario and raw summary is in the repository, and every run can be repeated on your own hardware. Corrections from the maintainers of any system are welcome.

Four contracts

Job frameworks

AwaObanpg-bossProcrastinateRiver

A job is claimed by one worker, retried with backoff and completed or failed individually.

Durable workflow

Absurd

Tasks run as checkpointed steps that replay after an interruption; driven here as one-step jobs through its worker.

Visibility queue

PGMQ

A message becomes invisible when read and reappears unless deleted; no per-job retry policy, scheduling or dead-letter handling beyond its archive.

Event queue (PgQ lineage)

PgQue

Consumers read batches cut by a ticker and acknowledge a whole batch in one statement. Driven here as competing consumers; batch acknowledgement is a lighter contract than per-job completion.

Throughput at saturation

How many 1 ms jobs per second does one worker process complete as it gets more workers?

Setup. Out of the box: the producer keeps 4,000 jobs queued; one worker process with 4, 16, 64 or 128 workers and a pool of workers + 8 connections; 30 s warmup, then 150 s measured. Tuned: each system at the settings its own documentation points to, within one budget for all: at most 4 processes, 128 handlers and 200 connections, the same 1 ms jobs, measured at one queue and at its recommended number of queues.

  • PgQue completes the most events (47,282/s tuned), but it acknowledges a batch at a time and its single producer was the limit: the queue stayed empty with Postgres at 1.1 cores, so treat it as a floor.
  • PGMQ peaks at 12,013/s with 16 workers. With more readers each pgmq.read scans past the others' in-flight rows, Postgres saturates and throughput falls (3,678/s at 128 workers; 5,710/s tuned over 16 queues).
  • Among job frameworks, tuned and sharded over 4 queues, River (10,905/s on 2.2 Postgres cores) and Awa (10,762/s on 4.0 cores) lead, then Oban (5,673/s), pg-boss (3,495/s, Postgres-bound), Absurd (1,625/s) and Procrastinate (1,472/s).
  • Configuration matters most for River (2,509/s at 128 workers with the adapter's 50 ms fetch cooldown) and Procrastinate (451/s on one process, 3.3× with four).
Reading it
  • PgQue and PGMQ provide lighter contracts than the job frameworks; compare them with each other, not with job frameworks.
  • Queue wait dominates claim latency here by design, so throughput is the figure to compare.
  • The tuned settings are listed below with their sources in each system's proposal; corrections from maintainers are welcome.

Latency at a steady 800 jobs/s

At a rate every system should sustain, how quickly does a job go from insert to completion?

Setup. 800 jobs/s offered, 32 workers, one process; 60 s warmup, then 300 s measured.

  • Awa, Oban and PGMQ pick up within 20 ms at p99; PgQue at 107 ms (its consumer's 100 ms poll); pg-boss at 410 ms.
  • River (634/s), Procrastinate (442/s) and Absurd (223/s) fell behind and their queues grew. River's adapter cooldown caps 32 workers at 640 jobs/s; Procrastinate and Absurd are limited per process (see saturation).
Reading it
  • A system that cannot keep up builds a backlog, and its latency grows for the whole run; those are marked with the rate they reached.

Cost of doing nothing

With workers running and no jobs, how much does each system cost the database?

Setup. One process with 8 workers after a short warmup; 10 minutes with nothing enqueued.

  • Oban, pg-boss, Absurd and River each cost Postgres about 16–24 transactions/s when idle; PGMQ costs 160/s from its polling consumers.
  • Awa keeps 276 MB of private Postgres memory while idle, against 18–59 MB for the others: each of its connections caches metadata and prepared plans for its 220 ring tables.
Reading it
  • Background polling, heartbeats and maintenance all show up here; they are paid on every deployment, busy or not.

PostgreSQL restart under load

When the database restarts mid-run, does the system come back and work off what accumulated?

Setup. Two processes, 400 jobs/s each, 16 workers each. 90 s baseline; Postgres stopped for 45 s and started again; 120 s recovery.

  • Every system came back to its pre-restart rate within the 5 s check after Postgres returned.
  • Absurd and Procrastinate get there only because their adapters restart the worker: both libraries stop the worker when the database goes away and expect a supervisor to start it again.
Reading it
  • Awa, Oban, pg-boss and River reconnect inside the running worker. PGMQ and PgQue are consumed by the adapters’ own loops, which reconnect.
  • Absurd's Python worker loop and Procrastinate's worker both stop when they lose the database, and expect a process supervisor (systemd, Kubernetes) to start them again. Their adapters restart the worker after 1 s, as a supervisor would. Without a supervisor, these workers stay stopped.
  • Zero after the restart means no jobs completed for the rest of the run.

Impact on a neighbouring application

Does a busy queue slow down unrelated transactions on the same server?

Setup. pgbench runs a TPC-B-like workload at a fixed 300 TPS on a separate database throughout. The queue runs at 0, 400, 1,600, then 400 jobs/s.

  • No system cut the neighbour's throughput (all within 1% of baseline).
  • With the queue at 4× load, the neighbour's p99 ranged from 2.4 ms (PgQue) and 5–7 ms (Procrastinate, Awa, River) to 18 ms (PGMQ).
Reading it
  • Latency is measured from each pgbench transaction's scheduled start, so it includes any queueing.

Logical replication alongside the queue

Does the queue coexist with CDC on the same database, including when the CDC consumer stops?

Setup. A FOR ALL TABLES publication, a pgoutput slot and a streaming consumer; 400 jobs/s. The consumer is frozen for 4 minutes, then resumes.

  • Awa at 1a53fd9e now gives every table a replica identity (0 tables without one, down from 19 on 0.6.17) but still leaves the most dead catalog tuples while the slot is stalled (11,374, down from 19,024; the others 20–214).
  • PGMQ has 2 and PgQue 5 tables without a replica identity, so logical replication of updates and deletes on them fails.
Reading it
  • A table without a replica identity rejects UPDATE and DELETE while a FOR ALL TABLES publication exists.
  • Dead catalog tuples pile up when a system creates or truncates tables while a stalled slot holds back catalog cleanup.

A herd of scheduled jobs

When 20,000 jobs come due at the same instant, how late do they start, and do immediate jobs suffer?

Setup. 20,000 jobs scheduled for one instant while a 200 jobs/s stream of immediate jobs keeps flowing; 16 workers.

  • PGMQ starts 20,000 simultaneous scheduled jobs within 1.9 s (p99), Awa within 12 s and Oban 21 s; Procrastinate 41 s, River 63 s, pg-boss 77 s, Absurd 137 s.
  • Immediate jobs keep flowing during the herd on Awa, Oban, Procrastinate and PGMQ (p99 under 50 ms); River's, pg-boss's and Absurd's wait seconds behind it. PgQue has no per-job scheduling.
Reading it
  • PgQue has no delayed delivery and is omitted from the herd figures.

A storm of failing jobs

When a third of jobs start failing, does useful work keep flowing?

Setup. 200 jobs/s, 16 workers. For 6 minutes 30% of new jobs fail once and 1% always fail; at most 5 attempts; each system's own backoff applies.

  • With 30% transient and 1% poison failures, every system kept completing the good jobs at the offered rate (132–138/s).
Reading it
  • Backoff policies differ by orders of magnitude between systems; that difference is part of what this measures.

Many queues

What does spreading the same load over 200 queues cost, and how does each system serve many queues?

Setup. One process. 200 jobs/s in total, round-robin over 1 queue and then over 200 queues (1 job/s per queue); 16 handlers and 24 connections requested; each system uses its own idiomatic way of serving many queues, the same in both runs; 4 minutes busy, then 3 minutes idle.

  • Every system kept up with 200 jobs/s over 200 queues.
  • Idle cost at 200 queues ranges from 0.02–0.05 Postgres cores (Oban, River, Procrastinate) to about 1 core (pg-boss, PgQue) and 1.7 cores (Absurd).
Reading it
  • Awa (weighted workers), Procrastinate (one worker over every queue), PGMQ and PgQue share 16 handlers across all 200 queues.
  • River, Oban, pg-boss and Absurd need at least one worker per queue, so they run 200 at 200 queues. That is part of the result.
  • Per-queue polling and listening connections multiply with the queue count.

Large job arguments

What happens with 64 KiB and 1 MiB arguments, and does the database shrink back afterwards?

Setup. Incompressible arguments. Saturation: one process, 64 workers, 2,000 jobs queued, 120 s. Backlog: about 8 GiB of 1 MiB jobs preloaded by four processes, then drained by 4 × 16 workers.

  • After draining an 8 GiB backlog of 1 MiB jobs, Awa's database is back to 21 MB; every other system keeps 7–8 GB (PGMQ 16 GB, archive included).
  • Worker memory while draining 1 MiB jobs: Oban 8.7 GB and pg-boss 5.3 GB, against 0.2–1 GB for the others.
Reading it
  • The saturation cells are bound by one producer connection, so they compare insert paths as much as workers.
  • PostgreSQL keeps a table's disk space after its rows are deleted; only systems that truncate or drop storage give it back without VACUUM FULL.

Moderate payloads over time

At a gentle rate with 64 KiB arguments, how much WAL is written and how much space is left behind?

Setup. 64 KiB incompressible arguments at 50 jobs/s for 5 minutes, then 3 minutes of draining.

  • Only Awa gives the space back: 22 MB after the run, where every other system keeps about 1.2 GB of TOAST until VACUUM FULL.
  • PGMQ writes about 2.5× the WAL per 64 KiB job of the others (205 KB), because acknowledging archives the message.

Long-running jobs

What does it cost to keep jobs running for 40 seconds each?

Setup. Two processes × 100 workers; 40 s jobs; 150 held, then a steady stream for 6 minutes.

  • Keeping 40 s jobs running costs between 4.6 KB/s of WAL (PGMQ) and 28 KB/s (Awa, whose lease heartbeats write more often).
  • Absurd (1,134), PGMQ (610), River (360) and Oban (298) ran some 40 s jobs more than once in this cell.
Reading it
  • Heartbeats and lease extensions are writes. A timeout shorter than the job causes duplicate execution.

Draining a backlog

How fast does a 200,000-job backlog drain, and how big is the database afterwards?

Setup. 200,000 jobs preloaded behind a gate, then drained by 64 workers, then 4 minutes idle.

  • PGMQ and PgQue drain a 200,000-job backlog at about 14,500 jobs/s; Awa at 5,154/s, Oban 4,206/s, pg-boss 2,510/s, River 1,226/s, Procrastinate 579/s; Absurd did not finish in 6 minutes.

A million abandoned claims

When an entire fleet dies holding about a million claimed jobs, how long until those jobs have run?

Setup. Four processes preload 1,000,000 jobs and claim them all while their handlers hold instead of running. All four are killed at once (SIGKILL); a fresh fleet of 4 × 128 workers starts, with 80 new jobs/s arriving. Every claim timeout is 300 s.

  • PgQue redelivers abandoned batches at once (16 s); among per-job systems PGMQ finishes in 5.9 min, pg-boss 14 min, Awa 20.5 min, Absurd 28.5 min and Procrastinate 32 min.
  • Oban's Lifeline rescued about a fifth of the jobs in 30 minutes (798,438 still unfinished).
  • Apart from Oban's unfinished jobs, none went missing; a few ran twice (Absurd 43, pg-boss 80, PGMQ 102, PgQue 12).
Reading it
  • Timeout-based systems cannot release a claim before its timeout, which has to exceed the longest job; the first rescue is mostly configuration. The rate after that is the system.
  • PgQue is a different contract, not a faster rescue. A consumer's claim is one open batch (here about 200,000 events each). The restarted consumer gets the same batch back at once, reads it in one query, runs the handlers and acknowledges the whole batch with one statement. The job frameworks write a state change for every one of the million jobs.
  • The completion curves include working the jobs off again, so the fresh fleet's speed bounds them.
  • River held no claims to abandon: with 250,000 workers per process its fetches (up to 5,500 jobs per queue) hit River's 10 s fetch timeout on the million-job table, so this setup measures nothing for River.

How the runs work

Measure the contract you need.

A common database does not create a common benchmark. Each system is driven through its public API by an adapter in this repository, with the same harness, workload and database.

01

Start with steady state.

Choose handler work, payload, offered load, pool budget and concurrency. Warm up before sampling; measure completion rate and end-to-end latency together with backlog.

02

Push past equilibrium.

Overload the producer, then reduce the load. Observe queue growth, recovery and whether retries consume capacity intended for useful work.

03

Make PostgreSQL uncomfortable.

Hold an old snapshot, introduce long transactions and watch vacuum, WAL and lock waits. A short clean-throughput cell misses those costs.

04

Break the process.

Kill a worker, interrupt a database connection, and restart PostgreSQL in a controlled environment. Account for work and external effects, rather than only observing a returning rate.

Six profiles are documentation-only.

PotatoQ, Graphile Worker, GoodJob, Que, Solid Queue and original PgQ are not presented as measured adapters. Adding them requires a version-pinned adapter and a comparable, fully specified run.

Keep the environment together.

Record the database/image, host allocation, system and SDK commits, batch sizes, connections, workload and test duration. Do not combine different environments or per-system best cases into one ranking.

Account for the contract.

A per-job completion, visibility-timeout acknowledgement and whole-batch event acknowledgement have different costs. Workflow checkpointing adds another boundary. Test the mechanism you will deploy.

Keep gaps visible.

Missing samples stay missing. Repeat runs, preserve failures, distinguish metric sampling from independent trials, and audit reconnect/shutdown behavior before attributing an adapter result to the entire product.
